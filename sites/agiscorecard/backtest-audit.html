Backtest Reality Check

Every backtesting platform helps you make a backtest. This one asks whether the one you made survives the multiple-testing problem.
Paste an equity curve or a column of returns, say how many variants you tried before keeping this one, and get four numbers: the deflated Sharpe ratio, the minimum track record length, the highest annual return your Sharpe permits at any leverage, and what turnover costs do to it. Nothing is uploaded — the arithmetic runs in this page.

Your track record

What the four numbers mean

Deflated Sharpe ratio. A Sharpe ratio does not know how many times you looked. Test 200 variants with no real edge and the best one still shows a respectable Sharpe, at a height that is predictable rather than mysterious. Bailey and López de Prado's deflated Sharpe subtracts that height first, then asks whether anything is left, widening the error bars when returns are negatively skewed or fat-tailed. Above 0.95 the record survives the correction; below it, the record is not distinguishable from the best of that many coin flips.

Minimum track record length. How many observations you would need before the measured Sharpe is separable from luck at all. A common and uncomfortable result: the record in hand is shorter than the record it would take to know.

The ceiling. Compound growth at leverage L is L·μ − L²σ²/2. That is a downward parabola: it peaks at the Kelly leverage and falls again above it, so the best annual return any leverage can reach is exp(S²/2) − 1 and depends on the Sharpe ratio alone. A 100% target needs a Sharpe of at least 1.177 at full Kelly, about 1.360 at half. At a Sharpe of 1.0 the ceiling is 64.9%.

The cost haircut. The edge that survives the first three tests still has to pay a spread on every rebalance. This is where a monthly-turnover strategy with a thin edge stops being one.

Run it yourself, or on many records

The same arithmetic ships as a single dependency-free Python file in this site's public repository, so you can check the implementation, run it on files, or put it in a pipeline:

python3 audit.py --equity curve.csv --trials 200 --target 1.0
python3 audit.py --selftest   # 30 assertions, offline

Source: tools/strategy-audit/audit.py in f-tiger/agi-site. Standard library only. The browser version above and the Python version are held to the same numbers by a parity test.

Worked example: the S&P 500 itself

Fourteen months of SPY has an annualised Sharpe of 1.63, which is high enough that 4x leverage would compound at 100% a year. Its deflated Sharpe, if you had arrived at it by trying 200 things, is 0.16. The index is not overfitted, of course — but that is the point. A number that looks like an edge and a number that is one are separated by the question the deflated Sharpe asks, and almost no backtest report asks it.

FAQ

What does a backtest audit actually check?

Four things, all arithmetic on the numbers you paste. Whether the Sharpe ratio survives being deflated for how many variants you tried, because searching 200 parameter sets over noise produces a good-looking best one by construction. How long a track record would have to be before that Sharpe is separable from luck. Whether the return target you have in mind is reachable at any leverage, since compound growth peaks at the Kelly fraction and falls above it, capping the best possible annual return at exp(S squared over 2) minus 1. And what turnover costs do to the edge. It never recommends a security and never sees your data.

Why is my Sharpe ratio not enough on its own?

Because a Sharpe ratio does not know how many times you looked. If you test 200 strategy variants that all have zero real edge, the best of them will still show an annualised Sharpe well above zero, and that height is predictable: it is roughly the expected maximum of 200 draws. The deflated Sharpe ratio of Bailey and Lopez de Prado subtracts that height before asking whether anything is left, and also widens the error bars when returns are negatively skewed or fat-tailed, because both make a Sharpe ratio flatter than it looks.

Is a 100% annual return possible?

Only with a sustained Sharpe ratio of at least 1.177 sized at full Kelly, or about 1.360 at half Kelly, before borrowing costs, fees and taxes. This is not a rule of thumb. Compound growth at leverage L is L times mu minus L squared sigma squared over two, a downward parabola that peaks at the Kelly leverage and falls again above it, so the highest annual return any leverage can reach is exp(S squared over 2) minus 1 and depends on the Sharpe ratio alone. At a Sharpe of 1.0 that ceiling is 64.9%, and at 0.6 it is 19.7%.

Does this tool tell me what to buy?

No, and it is built so that it cannot. It reads a column of numbers you already have and returns statistics about that column. It names no security, makes no forecast, and takes no position on whether you should invest in anything. A recommendation about a financial instrument is a regulated activity; a statistic about a dataset is not, and the line between them is the reason this tool is shaped the way it is.
