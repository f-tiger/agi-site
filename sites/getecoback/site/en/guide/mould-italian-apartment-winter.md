# Mould in Italian flats in winter: why it comes back, what stops it

> Muffa grows where indoor air meets a cold wall: 20 °C and 50 % in the room is already 73 % at a 14 °C wall. The physics, the Italian building details, what fixes it.

Canonical (HTML, zitierfähig): https://getecoback.com/en/guide/mould-italian-apartment-winter.html

**Short version:** muffa is not dirt and not bad luck. It grows wherever the humidity **right at a surface** stays above about 70 percent, and a room at a comfortable 20 °C and 50 percent is already at **73 percent at a wall of 14 °C**. Italian flats supply those cold walls reliably — masonry thermal bridges at corners, balcony slabs and window reveals, heated in bursts rather than continuously. Three levers change the number: **warm the surface, dry the air, make less moisture**. Cleaning is what you do once, after.

## The number on the hygrometer is not the number on the wall

Relative humidity is relative to temperature. Cool a parcel of air and its relative humidity rises even though it contains exactly the same water, because cold air can hold less. The air touching an outside wall is colder than the air in the middle of the room, so its relative humidity is higher — and mould grows on the wall, not in the middle of the room. The table gives the humidity at the surface for a room at 20 °C, computed with the standard Magnus formula (the same one our calculator below uses):

Room at 20 °C, hygrometer reads… | Wall at 17 °C | Wall at 14 °C | Wall at 12 °C | Wall at 10 °C |

40 % | 48 % | 58 % | 67 % | 76 % |

50 % | 60 % | **73 %** | 83 % | 95 % |

60 % | 72 % | 88 % | 100 % — water condenses | 100 % — water condenses |

Two things stand out. A wall at 14 °C — an ordinary reading for an uninsulated exterior wall in January — needs only **48 percent in the room** to reach 70 at the surface. And at 60 percent in the room, a 12 °C wall is not merely damp; the air at it has reached its dew point and water is forming. That is the wet patch that appears overnight behind furniture on an outside wall.

## Where Italian flats are cold: the ponte termico

Italian builders have a word for the exact spot where this happens, and it is worth knowing because it tells you where to look: the ponte termico, thermal bridge. In a typical Italian building — a reinforced-concrete frame filled in with brick, or older solid masonry — the concrete columns and beams, the balcony slab that runs straight through the wall, and the reveal around each window conduct heat outwards faster than the brick beside them. On the inside they are the coldest patches of wall, often by several degrees. Mould traces them faithfully: the vertical stripe on the corner where a column is, the line above a balcony door, the frame of a north-facing window.

Furniture makes it worse. A wardrobe against an outside wall stops the room's warm air from reaching that wall at all, so the surface behind it sits close to the outdoor temperature. Pulling it 5–10 cm off the wall is the cheapest fix on this page.

## The heating schedule makes it worse

Many Italian flats are heated by a shared condominium boiler on a fixed daily schedule, or by a gas caldaia that the household runs for a few hours morning and evening to keep the bill down. Either way the walls never get warm. Air heats in minutes; masonry takes hours, and a wall that is warmed for three hours and left for nine spends most of the day cold while the air in front of it carries the moisture of cooking, showers and people. Bedrooms are the classic case: unheated to save money, closed at night, one or two people breathing out water for eight hours, and a cold outside wall.

This is why the most effective heating change is not more heat but steadier heat — a room held at 16–18 °C continuously has warmer walls than one blasted to 22 °C for two hours.

## Three levers, in the order that works

Lever | What it does to the number | How |

**1. Make less moisture, get it out** | Lowers the room's humidity, so every wall improves at once | Lid on pans; extractor or window during and after showers; laundry dried in one closed room with a dehumidifier, not on radiators in the living room; windows fully open for 5–10 minutes several times a day instead of ajar for hours ([why the ajar window is the mould setting](https://getecoback.com/en/guide/tilt-and-turn-windows-winter-condensation.html)) |

**2. Dry the air** | Same effect as lever 1, mechanically, on days ventilation cannot do it | A dehumidifier sized to the room — [16–20 litres per day rated capacity for 20 m²](https://getecoback.com/en/guide/dehumidifier-20-sqm.html). Compressor models are fine in a heated room; in a cold, unheated room read [compressor vs desiccant](https://getecoback.com/en/guide/desiccant-vs-compressor-dehumidifier.html) first, because below about 12 °C the compressor type barely works |

**3. Warm the surface** | Only helps the wall it warms — but three degrees of wall temperature are worth about 13 points of surface humidity in the 50 % example above | Steady low heat rather than bursts; furniture off outside walls; an infrared panel aimed at the cold wall. One hard limit, explained in our [German infrared-versus-mould guide](https://getecoback.com/guide/infrarotheizung-gegen-schimmel.html): a surface can never be warmer than the room air, so above roughly 62 percent room humidity no amount of heating gets the wall under 60 — then it is lever 1 or 2 or nothing |

## Check your own wall

You need two readings: the room's temperature and humidity from any hygrometer, and the temperature of the coldest surface. An infrared thermometer (the kind pointed at a pan or a forehead) reads a wall from across the room; failing that, a cheap contact thermometer taped to the corner overnight. Enter the three numbers:

## Cleaning is not the fix — but here is how to do it once

Once the surface is dry and warmer, small patches can be cleaned. Use **one product only** — never mix a chlorine-based cleaner with vinegar or any acid, which releases chlorine gas — and follow the label rather than any recipe you find online; we deliberately give no concentrations. Ventilate, wear gloves, and do not dry-brush a patch (that spreads spores). A useful test from our German guide to [mould in bathroom grout](https://getecoback.com/guide/schimmel-bad-fugen.html): if the surface whitens evenly it was surface growth; if a grey shadow stays, the growth is in the material and the silicone or plaster needs replacing — after the humidity is fixed, or you will do it twice. Larger areas, anything that keeps returning within weeks, or mould in a home with small children or anyone with respiratory illness is a job for a professional, and this page is not medical advice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does the mould come back after I clean it?

Because cleaning removes the colony, not the conditions. Mould grows where the humidity right at the surface stays above roughly 70 percent, and that depends on how cold the wall is and how much water is in the air. If the wall is still cold and the air still damp in January, the same spot regrows. Fix the surface temperature or the moisture, then clean once.

### My hygrometer says 50 percent. How can there be mould?

The hygrometer measures the air in the middle of the room. Relative humidity rises as air cools, and the air in contact with a cold wall is colder than the room. At 20 degrees and 50 percent, the humidity right at a wall of 14 degrees is about 73 percent, already in the range where spores germinate. With a 14-degree wall the room only has to reach 48 percent for that wall to sit at 70.

### Should I heat more or ventilate more?

Both, for different reasons. Heating warms the wall: three degrees of wall temperature, from 14 to 17, take the surface humidity in that example from 73 to about 60 percent. Ventilating removes water: opening the windows fully for five to ten minutes several times a day swaps the damp air for drier outdoor air without cooling the walls the way a window left ajar does. Neither replaces the other.

### Compressor or desiccant dehumidifier for an Italian flat?

In a heated room at 16 to 20 degrees a compressor dehumidifier works well and costs less to run. In a room that is left unheated, a cold north-facing bedroom or a taverna in the basement, the air can be so cool that a compressor unit barely extracts anything; below about 12 degrees a desiccant model is the right tool. Our comparison of the two explains the dew-point reason.

### Will a dehumidifier from amazon.de work in Italy?

Electrically yes: both countries use 230 V. The plug is the question. Italy has its own three-pin Type L sockets alongside Type F (Schuko); a German appliance has a Schuko plug, which fits a Type F or a bipasso-Schuko socket but not an old Type L-only socket. Check the sockets in the room first; an adapter costs a few euros. Many amazon.de listings ship to Italy, but check amazon.it for local stock and delivery before ordering.
