# Tilt-and-Turn Windows in Winter: Tilted or Wide Open?

> Leaving a tilt-and-turn window tilted all winter is what grows mould around the frame. Why ten minutes wide open dries a flat, with the numbers.

Canonical (HTML, zitierfähig): https://getecoback.com/en/guide/tilt-and-turn-windows-winter-condensation.html

If you have moved into a flat in Germany, Austria, Switzerland or most of Central Europe, your windows tilt inwards at the top as well as swinging open — a **tilt-and-turn** window, Dreh-Kipp-Fenster. In summer that tilt position is genuinely useful, and it is how most people vent a portable air conditioner hose. In winter it is the single setting most likely to put mould around your window frame.

**Quick answer:** Don't leave the window on tilt through the winter. A tilted sash exchanges air slowly but chills the frame and the wall around the opening for hours, and the cold surface is where mould starts — not in the middle of the room. Open it **fully for 5–10 minutes, twice a day**, two windows at once if you can. Cold air is dry air, so a short full airing takes moisture out and the room warms back up quickly, while a tilted window mostly leaks heat.

## The cold corner decides, not the room

A hygrometer on your shelf reads the air in the middle of the room. Mould does not grow there. It grows on the coldest surface in the room — the window reveal, the lintel, the corner of an exterior wall, the patch of wall behind a wardrobe.

The reason is that relative humidity is relative to temperature. The same air, cooled, is closer to saturation. So warm room air that reads a perfectly comfortable value in the middle of the room can arrive at a cold surface already in the danger zone:

- Room air at **20 °C and 50 % relative humidity** — comfortable, nothing on the hygrometer to worry about — reaches about **73 % at a wall surface of 14 °C**.

- Spores germinate from roughly **70–80 % relative humidity at the surface**, which that wall has just crossed.

- At **20 °C and 60 %**, the dew point is about **12 °C**: any surface colder than that collects liquid water, not just damp.

This is why the tilted window is such a reliable mould machine. It does not flood the room with moisture; it quietly holds the masonry around the opening near outdoor temperature for hours at a time, and then ordinary indoor air does the rest against that cold strip. The visible result — black speckling that follows the frame — is the shape of the problem.

## Cold outside air dries your flat

The common objection is that it is cold and wet outside, so letting that air in must make things damper. It does the opposite, because cold air physically cannot hold much water.

Outside | Water it actually carries | Same air once it is 20 °C indoors |

−5 °C at 90 % RH | ≈ 3.1 g/m³ | ≈ 16 % RH |

0 °C at 80 % RH | ≈ 3.9 g/m³ | ≈ 21 % RH |

5 °C at 80 % RH | ≈ 5.4 g/m³ | ≈ 30 % RH |

10 °C at 80 % RH | ≈ 7.5 g/m³ | ≈ 42 % RH |

Figures are the Magnus formula for saturation vapour pressure, the standard approximation used in building physics. Relative humidity is the ratio of the water vapour actually present to what the air could hold at that temperature, so warming air without adding water lowers the ratio: 0 °C air at 80 % keeps its vapour pressure and lands near 21 % once it is 20 °C indoors. The grams-per-cubic-metre column is what that outdoor air physically carries. You can check the arithmetic yourself — [how we work](https://getecoback.com/en/how-we-recommend.html#check-the-maths).

That "room to absorb" is the whole mechanism. Winter air comes in dry, picks up the moisture your shower, cooking, laundry and breathing put into the flat, and you throw it back out at the next airing. A window on tilt does a weak, continuous version of this while cooling the structure — the worst combination of the two.

## The four numbers worth holding on to

- **40–60 % RH** in the room: the green band.

- **60–70 %**: grey zone, worth watching, especially first thing in the morning.

- **Repeatedly above 70 %**: act — more airing, less moisture at source, and a dehumidifier if the first two are not enough.

- **Below 16 °C** in a bedroom: relative humidity climbs into the critical range even with no extra moisture source, which is why an unheated bedroom with the door shut is a classic mould room.

Going too far the other way is also possible: a hygrometer sitting under 40 % through a frost period means dry air, not safety. We put the running cost of fixing that on the [humidifier electricity page](https://getecoback.com/guide/luftbefeuchter-stromverbrauch.html) (German).

## The routine that replaces the tilt

- **Twice a day, 5–10 minutes, sash fully open.** Two windows on opposite sides at once (Querlüften) does it fastest.

- **Turn the radiator down while the window is open**, back up when you close it. A thermostatic valve left running will otherwise heat the street.

- **Air immediately after showering, cooking and drying laundry** — those are the big moisture events, and the moisture is easiest to remove before it spreads.

- **Leave a gap behind furniture on exterior walls.** A wardrobe flat against a cold wall makes its own cold spot; we go through that failure mode on [why mould keeps coming back](https://getecoback.com/guide/schimmel-wand-kommt-wieder.html) (German).

Honest limit: none of this fixes a cold surface that is cold for structural reasons. If one patch stays far colder than the rest of the same wall, no airing schedule will save it, and that is a conversation with whoever owns the building rather than a shopping problem. We are not building surveyors and this page is not legal advice about a tenancy.

## Questions people actually ask

### Should I leave my window tilted in winter?

Not all day. A tilted sash exchanges air slowly while cooling the frame and the wall around the opening for hours, and a cold surface is where mould starts. Open the window fully for 5 to 10 minutes instead, twice a day, ideally two windows at once so the air crosses the flat.

### Does airing in winter make the flat damp, since outside air is cold and wet?

No, the opposite. Cold air carries very little water. Outdoor air at 0 degrees Celsius and 80 percent humidity holds about 3.9 grams of water per cubic metre; warmed to 20 degrees indoors that same air sits at roughly 21 percent relative humidity, so it has plenty of room to pick up moisture and carry it out at the next airing.

### My hygrometer says 50 percent, so why is there mould in the corner?

Because a hygrometer reads the middle of the room and mould grows at the coldest surface. Room air at 20 degrees Celsius and 50 percent relative humidity reaches about 73 percent when it touches a wall at 14 degrees, which is already in the range where spores germinate. The number that decides is the humidity at the cold spot, not the room average.

### How long should I open the window, and does the flat get cold?

Five to ten minutes with the sash fully open is enough to swap the air in a normal room. Walls, floor and furniture hold their heat over that time and warm the fresh air back up quickly, which is why brief full airing costs less heat than a window left on tilt for hours. Turn the radiator down while the window is open and back up afterwards.
