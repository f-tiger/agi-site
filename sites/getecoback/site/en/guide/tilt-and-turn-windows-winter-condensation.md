# Tilt-and-Turn Windows in Winter: Tilted or Wide Open?

> At 20°C and 50% humidity indoors, outside air at 5°C and 90% can dry the room. At 14°C and 95% it adds moisture. See when opening the window helps.

Canonical (HTML, zitierfähig): https://getecoback.com/en/guide/tilt-and-turn-windows-winter-condensation.html

If you find water on the inside of a window in the morning, start with room temperature and humidity. A cold pane can collect condensation even when the rest of the room seems dry.

**Quick answer:** Airing helps remove moisture when the incoming air contains less water vapour at the same temperature than the room air. Take a temperature and relative humidity reading indoors and outdoors at about the same time. A rain forecast alone cannot answer the question.

## The cold corner decides, not the room

A hygrometer on your shelf reads the air in the middle of the room. Mould does not grow there. It grows on the coldest surface in the room — the window reveal, the lintel, the corner of an exterior wall, the patch of wall behind a wardrobe.

The reason is that relative humidity is relative to temperature. The same air, cooled, is closer to saturation. So warm room air that reads a perfectly comfortable value in the middle of the room can arrive at a cold surface already in the danger zone:

- Room air at **20 °C and 50 % relative humidity** — comfortable, nothing on the hygrometer to worry about — reaches about **73 % at a wall surface of 14 °C**.

- Spores germinate from roughly **70–80 % relative humidity at the surface**, which that wall has just crossed.

- At **20 °C and 60 %**, the dew point is about **12 °C**: any surface colder than that collects liquid water, not just damp.

A window left on tilt for hours can chill the frame and reveal. Moisture can then persist on those cold surfaces. One room reading cannot identify the cause of patches around a frame.

## When will outdoor air help dry a room?

Rain and high outdoor relative humidity can coincide with good drying conditions indoors. Compare outdoor air warmed to room temperature with the actual indoor humidity.

| Outside | Water it actually carries | Same air once it is 20 °C indoors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| −5 °C at 90 % RH | ≈ 3.1 g/m³ | ≈ 16 % RH |
| 0 °C at 80 % RH | ≈ 3.9 g/m³ | ≈ 21 % RH |
| 5 °C at 80 % RH | ≈ 5.4 g/m³ | ≈ 30 % RH |
| 10 °C at 80 % RH | ≈ 7.5 g/m³ | ≈ 42 % RH |

Figures are the Magnus formula for saturation vapour pressure, the standard approximation used in building physics. Relative humidity is the ratio of the water vapour actually present to what the air could hold at that temperature, so warming air without adding water lowers the ratio: 0 °C air at 80 % keeps its vapour pressure and lands near 21 % once it is 20 °C indoors. The grams-per-cubic-metre column is what that outdoor air physically carries. You can check the arithmetic yourself — [how we work](https://getecoback.com/en/how-we-recommend.html#check-the-maths).

Two worked examples start with the same room at **20 °C and 50% relative humidity**.

- **Outside at 5 °C and 90% RH:** warm that air to 20 °C and its relative humidity is about **34%**. Exchanging air can carry moisture out of this room.

- **Outside at 14 °C and 95% RH:** at 20 °C the incoming air would be about **65%** RH. Exchanging air brings more moisture into this room.

The calculation compares two air samples at equal pressure. Airflow, wet washing and cold surfaces also affect what happens in the room. Keep background vents clear and run mechanical ventilation as instructed. Persistent damp patches or a leak call for checking the cause. [Energy Saving Trust guidance on draughts and ventilation](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/draught-proofing/).

If washing has to dry indoors, [compare costs for the same fully dried load](https://getecoback.com/en/guide/dehumidifier-drying-clothes-cost.html#laundry-check) using your own electricity rate. If a rack and ventilation get the job done, keep using them.

## The four numbers worth holding on to

- **40–60 % RH** in the middle of the room: also check cold surfaces for condensation.

- **60–70 %**: grey zone, worth watching, especially first thing in the morning.

- **Repeatedly above 70 %**: check sources of moisture and air exchange. For drying, bring in extra outdoor air when the comparison with room air supports it.

- **Below 16 °C** in a bedroom: as the same air cools, its relative humidity rises. Whether water condenses on a cold wall also depends on the moisture content and the surface temperature.

Going too far the other way is also possible: a hygrometer sitting under 40 % through a frost period means dry air, not safety. We put the running cost of fixing that on the [humidifier electricity page](https://getecoback.com/guide/luftbefeuchter-stromverbrauch.html) (German).

## The routine that replaces the tilt

- **Where the window opens wide safely, try a brief airing.** Five to ten minutes can be a starting point. Wind, opening size and cross-ventilation affect how much air changes. After closing, check indoor humidity again once the temperature is comparable.

- **Turn the radiator down while the window is open**, back up when you close it. A thermostatic valve left running will otherwise heat the street.

- **Manage moisture from showers, cooking and drying laundry indoors.** Use any installed extract ventilation as intended. When considering opening a window to help the room dry, compare the indoor and outdoor air.

- **Leave a gap behind furniture on exterior walls.** A wardrobe flat against a cold wall makes its own cold spot; we go through that failure mode on [why mould keeps coming back](https://getecoback.com/guide/schimmel-wand-kommt-wieder.html) (German).

Honest limit: none of this fixes a cold surface that is cold for structural reasons. If one patch stays far colder than the rest of the same wall, no airing schedule will save it, and that is a conversation with whoever owns the building rather than a shopping problem. We are not building surveyors and this page is not legal advice about a tenancy.

## Questions people actually ask

### Should I leave my window tilted in winter?

A window left tilted for hours can cool the frame and reveal. If the incoming air is drier, opening wide briefly can take moisture out. Wind, opening size and cross-ventilation affect the time needed. If your home has mechanical ventilation, follow its operating instructions.

### Does airing in winter make the flat damp, since outside air is cold and wet?

It depends on both air samples. Measure temperature and relative humidity inside and outside. If the outdoor humidity adjusted to room temperature is lower than the indoor reading, ventilation can remove moisture. If it is higher, the incoming air brings in more water vapour.

### My hygrometer says 50 percent, so why is there mould in the corner?

Because a hygrometer reads the middle of the room and mould grows at the coldest surface. Room air at 20 degrees Celsius and 50 percent relative humidity reaches about 73 percent when it touches a wall at 14 degrees, which is already in the range where spores germinate. The number that decides is the humidity at the cold spot, not the room average.

### How long should I open the window, and does the flat get cold?

Five to ten minutes with a window wide open in winter is a possible starting point. Wind, opening size and new moisture from activities affect how long you need. Check again when the indoor temperature is comparable. If moisture persists, investigate the source and the home’s ventilation.
