Keep warm while working from home

Keeping a person warm and heating the fabric of a room are different jobs. A low-wattage product does not automatically do both.

EcoBack editorial team · 4 October 2026 · Source-based guidance, no hands-on product tests

Read the guide ↓

Start with the workspace

Move away from a cold pane or a draught where possible. Add clothing and floor insulation under your feet without blocking vents or creating trip hazards. Check the existing heating schedule and whether furniture obstructs a radiator. These reversible changes can improve comfort before another appliance is needed.

Compare equal tasks

A heated throw warms a person; a fan heater warms air. Comparing rated watts alone does not establish equal comfort. An illustrative 100 W for four hours at 0.35 per kWh costs 0.14 in the chosen currency; 2,000 W for the same time costs 2.80. A thermostatic heater may cycle, so measured whole-session kWh is better than assuming full power throughout.

Keep the room healthy

Personal warmth does not prevent cold walls, condensation or frost damage. Maintain suitable room conditions, ventilate appropriately and monitor humidity, especially in occupied rooms. A blanket must not conceal a heating fault that needs repair. Needs differ for people with limited movement or heat sensitivity.

Use electrical warmth safely

Follow the specific product’s instructions on folding, moisture, cleaning, unattended use and placement. Do not use damaged cables or improvise repairs. Check local electrical compatibility and safety instructions before buying. For people with reduced temperature sensation, establish suitability rather than simply choosing a hotter setting.

Common questions

Can a heated blanket replace all room heating?

No. Personal warmth does not adequately heat cold building surfaces or provide frost protection.

Is a lower wattage always cheaper?

At equal run time it uses less electricity, but it may provide a different service. Compare the task and actual use.
