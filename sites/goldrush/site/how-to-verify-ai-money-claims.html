Step 2 — check the five red flags

Each one marks a claim unverifiable, not necessarily false — which is precisely why the burden of proof stays on the seller:

  1. Screenshots as proof. Trivially faked, never auditable, and even genuine ones show a moment, not a track record.
  2. Revenue without the denominator. "$10k/month" means nothing without the traffic, ad spend, or hours that produced it.
  3. Backtests sold as track records. Try enough variants on the same history and a spectacular in-sample result is guaranteed — the graded evidence.
  4. The seller earns from the tool, not the method. The picks-and-shovels tell: their revenue arrives whether or not yours does.
  5. Testimonials where a ledger should be. A method that works produces a ledger; a method that sells produces testimonials.

Step 3 — the decisive test

Ask for the falsifiable live ledger: entries timestamped before outcomes, all results graded including losses, misses never deleted, methodology fixed in advance, net of costs. It is cheap to publish and impossible to fake retroactively — which is why its absence is informative. A seller who meets it deserves your attention; a seller who offers anything else instead has answered your question.

Is a verified funding round evidence that users make money?

No — and this is the most common confusion in the AI gold rush.
