The Doll Shop Scam-Check

Built from documented complaint threads, chargeback cases and counterfeit reports — the recurring patterns in them, not a total we counted. Run it against any shop before you enter a card number — including ones we recommend.

Four checks catch most fraudulent doll shops. A discount steeper than about 15% off factory list price, because authorised vendors have no room for more. No PayPal or credit-card option, because both give you a dispute window. No named factory you can ask whether the shop is authorised. And no written promise of photos of your actual doll before dispatch. Any single failure is enough to walk away.

The counterintuitive rule that catches most scams: doll factories set minimum pricing, and legitimate vendors sit only ~10–15% below list price. So a "70% OFF" flash sale isn't a deal — a discount that big is itself the scam signal. If the same doll is half price somewhere, that somewhere ships counterfeits (or nothing).

Which warning signs mean you should leave immediately?

  • Only bank wire, Western Union, crypto or gift cards. No legitimate doll vendor refuses buyer-protected payment.
  • Prices roughly half of every other shop. See the rule above — that's a counterfeit signal, not a bargain.
  • They refuse pre-shipment factory photos. The single strongest predictor of "the doll won't match the pictures."
  • Photos answer to reverse-image search on other sites. Stolen catalog images are the counterfeit starter kit.
  • Anything childlike anywhere in the catalog. Illegal in most jurisdictions and a marker of criminal operations. Leave and report.

What scams only show up after you have paid?

These are not in the ten checks above because you cannot see them from the product page. They come from documented buyer complaints, and each has a question that defuses it before you hand over money.

If one of them has already happened to you, the checks are the wrong tool: see what recourse survives after you have paid, which is sourced from the statute and the Financial Ombudsman rather than from forum advice. And for the one vendor we earn from, the evidence — including the bad parts — is published: the YourDoll evidence file.

  • "Ships from a local warehouse" — when it doesn't. Buyers have reported shops advertising domestic dispatch to imply no customs, then shipping from China anyway. Ask: "Which country does my order physically ship from, and what will the customs declaration say?" Get it in writing.
  • Duty collected, then charged again at the border. The nastier version of the DDP question: a shop bills you for taxes at checkout, does not remit them, and the carrier bills you a second time on arrival. Ask: "Is this DDP, and will you send me the customs entry or payment reference once it ships?" A vendor that genuinely paid can produce one.
  • Restocking fees on something never stocked. Buyers report being charged a flat fee (reports mention figures around $150) to cancel a made-to-order doll that had not been built or shipped. Ask: "What exactly can I be charged if I cancel before production starts?" Read the answer against their written policy.
  • Shipping methods you did not choose, billed to you. Reports include several hundred dollars added for air freight the buyer never selected. Ask: "Is shipping included at the quoted price, and can it be changed or upcharged later without my approval?"

Sources for these patterns: published customer reviews on Trustpilot and comparable review platforms, read July 2026. We name the behaviours rather than maintaining a blacklist, because shop names churn and behaviours do not.

How do you check the doll you received is authentic?

Major factories run anti-counterfeit systems — for example, WM Doll ships a scratch-card authenticity code you verify on the factory's official website. If you ordered a brand-name doll, use the factory's checker on arrival day, while your payment-dispute window is fresh.

More of the questions this raises — what factory photos are, what the card statement says, what customs now costs — are answered in the buyer FAQ.

Already scammed — what recourse do you actually have?

  1. Stop talking, start documenting. Screenshot the listing, your order, all chats. Vendors that stall with "please wait, remake coming" are running out your dispute clock.
  2. PayPal: open a dispute — you have 180 days from payment. Escalate to a claim; "item significantly not as described" fits counterfeit dolls exactly.
  3. Credit card: file a chargeback with the same evidence. Don't accept a partial-refund-for-5-star-review deal — that's review extortion, and you lose your leverage.
  4. Report the shop to the FTC (US), your card network, and warn others in owner communities.
