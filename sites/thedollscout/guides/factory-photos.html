Guides / Factory Photos

The factory photos arrived. Here is how to actually read them.

Every buying guide — ours included — says the same four words: get factory photos. Almost none says what to do when they arrive. This page is that answer: the five checks, what variance is normal, and the one photo that proves the doll is yours.

The short answer: check the photos against your order confirmation, not your memory — head sculpt, body and height, skin tone name, eye and nail colour, and every visible paid option. Ask for one photo with your order number on paper in the frame. Approve in writing only when all five match. Wrong now is a production correction; wrong after dispatch is an international freight dispute.

Why do factory photos matter more than any other check?

Made-to-order means there is exactly one moment when the thing you paid for exists but has not shipped. After You Order covers why that checkpoint matters; this page covers how to use it. Two facts set the stakes. First, a correction before crating is the factory repainting a face or swapping a head — their cost, days. After dispatch it is return freight on a 30–60 lb crate across a border, with the vendor holding your written approval. Second, your approval message becomes evidence: in a later “significantly not as described” dispute, it is the document both sides quote. Write it like something you may need to show a bank.

How do you prove the photos are of your doll?

A photo of the right model is not a photo of your doll. Two checks close that gap:

  • Order number on paper, in the frame. Ask for one photo with your order number handwritten on a sheet of paper next to the doll. It costs the factory nothing, and a legitimate vendor has no reason to refuse. A refusal is information.
  • Reverse-image-search the photos. The scam checklist applies this to listing photos before you pay; apply it again here. A “factory photo” that already appears on other shops or forums was not taken for your order. Mismatched backgrounds, flooring or lighting between shots in the same set deserve the same question.

What can a factory photo not tell you?

Honesty about the limits, because a checkpoint that claims too much protects nobody:

  • Anything internal. Gel versus hollow chest, skeleton tightness, loose joints — invisible in a still photo. If you paid for an internal option, ask for a short video of the area being pressed. We could not verify that every factory will provide one; asking costs nothing and the answer, either way, goes in your records.
  • Material feel, smell and true colour in daylight. None of these survives a camera. This is why the photo checkpoint does not replace the arrival routine — film the unboxing before you open the crate, because the photo approval covers what was made, not what was shipped.
  • Weight. The photo shows the doll; it does not show you lifting it. That decision belonged before the order.

What should you do if something looks wrong?

Reply before approving, name the exact discrepancy (“order states tone X, production sheet states tone Y”), and ask for corrected photos after the fix. Do not approve “with a note” — a conditional approval reads as an approval in every dispute that follows. And treat two pressure patterns as red flags, because they are the same shape as the stalling moves documented on the scam checklist: “approve now and we will fix it if it arrives wrong” (it moves the correction from their side of the border to yours), and a small discount to approve as-is when you did not ask for one (it converts a production error into your written acceptance).

If the vendor refuses photos outright or stalls past their own stated lead time, your leverage is no longer the checkpoint — it is the payment. The dispute clocks started when you paid, not when the doll arrives; check what your payment method still covers and act inside those windows rather than waiting out another promise.

Frequently asked questions

What should I check in factory photos before approving?

Check five things against your order confirmation, not against your memory: the head sculpt number or name, the body and its height, the skin tone name as it appears on the factory's swatch chart, the eye and nail colours you selected, and every paid option that is visible in a photo, such as standing feet or articulated fingers. Then ask for one photo with your order number handwritten on paper in the frame, and approve in writing only when all five match. Anything wrong at this stage is a production correction; the same thing wrong after dispatch is an international freight dispute.

Why does the skin tone look different in factory photos?

Factory photos are usually taken under bright workshop lighting against plain backgrounds, while listing images are studio-lit or edited renders — so some apparent difference in shade is normal and is not by itself grounds to reject. Judge the tone by its name instead: your order confirmation states a named tone from the factory's swatch chart, and the vendor can confirm in writing which named tone was applied. A tone that looks warmer or cooler under different light is lighting; a different tone name on the production sheet is the wrong doll.

How do I know the factory photos are really my doll?

Ask for one photo with your order number handwritten on paper, placed in the frame with the doll. It costs the factory nothing and a legitimate vendor has no reason to refuse. Separately, run the photos through a reverse image search — the same check our scam checklist applies to listing photos — because a photo that already appears on other shops or forums was not taken for your order. Photos with inconsistent backgrounds, lighting or flooring between shots deserve the same question.

What if the vendor refuses to send factory photos?

Do not approve dispatch, and put the request in writing so the refusal is on record. A written factory-photo policy is one of the ten checks on our pre-payment scam checklist precisely because refusal after payment is a documented complaint pattern. If the vendor stalls, remember that your dispute windows started at payment, not at delivery — check what your payment method still covers and act inside those windows rather than waiting out another promise.
