How do you clean a TPE or silicone doll?

Last updated 26 July 2026 · every number sourced, or marked unverified

Material care is where good dolls die early. Here's the whole routine, by material, plus the storage mistakes that cause most damage.

The number that actually decides this: published care guidance puts routine cleaning and drying at roughly 15–30 minutes after each use, before any of the monthly work below. Nobody asks you to commit to "a routine" in the abstract — they are asking for that half hour, repeatedly, for as long as you own it. If the honest answer is that you will skip it, buy silicone rather than TPE: TPE is porous, and skipped drying is how mould starts.

How do you clean a TPE doll?

  1. Clean after each use. Mild antibacterial soap and warm (never hot) water. Pat dry with a soft towel — never rub hard, never use a hair dryer on hot. TPE is porous: thorough drying is the anti-mold step people skip.
  2. Powder monthly. After the skin is fully dry, dust with pure cornstarch (or renewal powder). This keeps TPE from getting tacky and attracting lint.
  3. Oil every 1–3 months. A light coat of mineral oil (baby oil) keeps TPE supple and prevents drying cracks. More often in dry climates.
  4. Protect from heat and dye. Keep away from radiators, sunlight, hot cars. Wash dark clothing before dressing the doll — dye transfer stains are the most common cosmetic damage and are hard to reverse.

How do you clean a silicone doll?

  1. Clean after each use with mild soap and water — silicone is non-porous, and it is the material that came out clean at 24 hours in the study below.
  2. Powder occasionally to keep the surface silky. No oiling required.
  3. Avoid silicone-based lubricants — they degrade silicone skin. Water-based only (a good rule for TPE too).

What does cleaning not remove?

Everything above is about keeping the doll intact. Hygiene is a separate question, and this page used to answer it only in terms of mould. That was incomplete, so here is the part we were missing.

The closest published measurement we could find is a 2014 study in Sexually transmitted infections which swabbed two vibrators used by twelve women — one made of thermoplastic elastomer, the same family as TPE doll skin, and one of silicone — before cleaning, immediately after cleaning with a commercially available cleaner, and 24 hours later. HPV DNA was still detectable on the TPE shaft 24 hours after cleaning in 40% of the samples that had been positive immediately after. On the silicone shaft at 24 hours it was detectable in none.

The full set of figures the abstract reports, with the raw counts, because the denominator changes at the last sampling point and that is where the number is most often misquoted:

Sampling pointThermoplastic elastomerSilicone
After vaginal use, before cleaning89% (8 of 9)67% (6 of 9)
Immediately after cleaning56% (5 of 9)44% (4 of 9)
24 hours after cleaning40% (2 of the 5 still positive)none

The first two rows are shares of all nine samples; the third is a share of the samples that were still positive immediately after cleaning, not of all nine. Quoting “40% vs none” alongside the first two rows without saying so overstates the gap. The denominators are the abstract's own: twelve women took part, but the percentages are calculated on nine samples per device. The abstract does not say why, and the full text is paywalled, so we have not resolved it.

That is a real material difference, measured rather than asserted, and it is the concrete version of the porosity claim this site has been making all along. But it is a small study and the honest limits matter as much as the finding:

  • Twelve participants, two devices. Small.
  • Vibrators, not dolls. The material family is shared; the surface area, the shape and the cleaning access are not.
  • Detecting viral DNA is not the same as detecting infectious virus, and it is not proof that transmission occurred. The authors' own wording is that it "supports the potential for" transmission.
  • One commercial cleaner, in 2014.
  • The study is about shared use between people.

What it changes in practice. If you are the only person who will ever use the doll, this mostly is not your problem — your own flora is what is being redeposited. It becomes a real consideration in exactly two situations, and both are common enough to plan for: a doll shared between partners, and a doll bought second-hand. On the resale question the evidence points one way — a used TPE doll cannot be assumed cleaned to a hygienic state by its previous owner, and no published protocol exists that would let you verify it.

That last gap is not us hedging. The same study's authors state that at the time of publication there were no evidence-based cleaning recommendations for insertable products, and called for them to be created. We could not find any published since. So when a vendor or a forum post tells you a specific product or method sterilises TPE, ask what that claim is based on, because we could not find the study it would need.

Source: Anderson TA et al., "A study of human papillomavirus on vaginally inserted sex toys, before and after cleaning, among women who have sex with women and men", Sexually transmitted infections, 2014 (PMID 24739872, doi:10.1136/sextrans-2014-051558). We read the abstract in full; the full text is paywalled and we have not read it, so everything above is drawn from the abstract's own reported figures.

How should you store a doll without damaging it?

  • Best: hanging kit in a closet (neck-bolt hook + reinforced rod — a standard closet rod bends under doll weight, so use a heavy-duty rod or a freestanding rack).
  • Acceptable: lying flat on a soft, breathable surface, position changed periodically. Long-term pressure on one spot creates permanent flat spots and compression marks.
  • Never: tightly folded joints for weeks (skeleton stress + skin creases), damp storage (mold, especially TPE), direct sunlight (fading), or leaning poses (slow deformation).
  • Torsos: a clean, dry drawer or lockable case — one reason we recommend them to first-timers.

What repairs can you do yourself?

  • Small TPE tears: TPE solvent glue, applied thinly to both faces of the tear, pressed and left to cure. A $15 kit handles most minor damage — keep one on hand.
  • Silicone damage: silicone-specific adhesive; harder to make invisible. Deep damage on an expensive doll is worth a professional repair quote.
  • Loose finger wires: common, minor and fixable — owner communities have well-worn walkthroughs.

The supplies this routine actually needs

Nothing here is doll-specific except the repair glue, and none of it needs to come from a doll shop at doll-shop prices. Cornstarch is cornstarch. These open Amazon searches rather than single products, because stock and sellers churn and a dead link helps nobody.

Skip the upsells: "special" TPE renewal powders are usually cornstarch with a markup, and scented cleaners add perfume you do not want against porous skin. The only items worth paying up for are the repair glue and a rack that genuinely holds the weight.
