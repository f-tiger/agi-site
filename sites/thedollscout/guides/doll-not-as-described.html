Guides / Not as Described

The doll arrived, and it isn't what you ordered. The next 72 hours.

Disputes get decided on paperwork, and the strongest paperwork is the kind assembled in the first three days — usually by someone who didn't yet know they were assembling it. This page is the plan: what to document before you touch anything, the message that opens the dispute properly, the ladder of remedies worth asking for, and the point at which it stops being a conversation with the vendor.

The short answer: document first, negotiate second. Photograph the discrepancy as found, keep the crate, screenshot the listing before it changes, and check the difference against your order confirmation — not your memory of the photos. Then send one written message: exact discrepancy, evidence attached, one specific remedy, one reply-by date. Your payment clocks are probably still running — for goods not as described the chargeback window runs from receipt, not payment — but evidence does not keep the way deadlines do.

Why 72 hours, when the dispute windows are longer?

Not because a rule says so — the payment windows below are measured in months, and we found no universal rule that voids a late complaint. Three quieter reasons. Evidence degrades fast at the start: packaging gets discarded, the doll gets handled, cleaned and posed, and each of those makes “it arrived like this” harder to show. Listings change: the page you bought from can be edited after you complain, so the screenshot is only conclusive if it predates the complaint. And some vendors' own terms set short reporting windows for transit damage — check yours, because that clause, where it exists, is the one deadline on this page we cannot read for you. A same-day written report with photographs is a different kind of document from an email sent a fortnight later.

What should you do first when a doll arrives not as described?

Document before you negotiate, and document before you handle the doll any further. Photograph the discrepancy exactly as you found it, keep the crate and all packaging, and save your continuous unboxing video if you filmed one — our After You Order guide explains why that recording is the single strongest piece of evidence you can hold. Then screenshot the listing you bought from, including its photos and stated options, because listings can change after you complain. Only then write to the vendor.

Is a colour or seam difference really “not as described”?

Not always, and claiming it loosely weakens the claims that are real. Skin tone that reads warmer or cooler than the listing render is usually lighting — renders are edited, and the check that settles it is the tone name on your order confirmation, not the picture. Visible mould seams and small hand-finishing differences are normal manufacturing. A different head sculpt, a different body or height, a missing paid option, or a tone name that differs from the one you ordered are configuration errors, and those are what a not-as-described claim is measured against: the written order, not your memory of the photos.

The full variance table — what is normal, what is grounds to reject — is on the factory-photos guide, because the same judgement applies at both checkpoints. The difference is leverage: before dispatch a wrong tone was the factory's correction; now the same error is a cross-border dispute. If you approved factory photos that showed the error, say so in your own records and expect the vendor to quote your approval — it is the document both sides reach for.

How do you write the first message to the vendor?

One message, in writing, through a channel that leaves a record. Name the discrepancy precisely, attach the evidence, ask for one specific remedy, give one date. Vague complaints invite vague replies; a message shaped like a claim gets treated like one.

Copy-paste report (edit the brackets):

"Order #____, delivered [date]. The doll received does not match the order confirmation: ordered [head/body/tone/option: X], received [Y]. Attached: photos of the item as delivered, my order confirmation, [continuous unboxing video]. I am requesting [replacement of the incorrect part / replacement / refund of ____] and a reply by [date, 5–7 days out]. If I have not heard back by then, I will open a dispute with my payment provider within its window."

The last sentence is not a threat, it is a fact about process — and it tells a professional vendor you know the process. Keep the tone flat. Anger reads as negotiable; paperwork does not.

What remedy should you actually ask for?

International return freight on a 30–60 lb crate is the worst outcome for both sides — which is exactly your negotiating room. Ask down this ladder:

  • Replacement of the wrong part. A wrong or defective head is a part; heads ship in ordinary parcels. This is the cheapest correct fix and the one a legitimate vendor can most easily say yes to.
  • Replacement of the unit — for errors a part cannot fix: wrong body, wrong height, wrong tone. Expect production time again; get the new lead time in writing.
  • A partial refund you priced yourself. Reasonable for a liveable defect. Decide what the defect is worth to you before the vendor names a number first.
  • Full return and refund. The last resort it appears to be: you pay or fight over return freight, and the crate matters — return shipping without the original packaging is materially worse. This is where the payment-provider route below often becomes the realistic path instead.

Should you accept a partial refund?

For a minor, liveable defect, a partial refund can be the sensible outcome — international return freight is the worst path for both sides. Two cautions before you accept. Never trade a refund for changing or removing a review; that swap removes your leverage and is a documented pattern in this category. And if you might still escalate, ask your card issuer first how accepting a partial refund affects a later dispute — we could not verify a universal rule, and the answer belongs in writing from them, not from the vendor.

Can you do a chargeback for a doll that doesn't match the listing?

Sometimes — but a chargeback is a card-scheme process, not a legal right, and your bank does not have to raise one. The Financial Ombudsman Service describes the window as usually around 120 days, and for goods that arrived not as described it runs from when you received them, not from when you paid. UK buyers who paid by credit card may also have Section 75 rights on items priced over £100 and up to £30,000, which is a legal claim against the card issuer. Escalate when the conversation stops being one: no reply by your stated date, a refusal that ignores the evidence, or stalling past the vendor's own stated timelines. Bring everything the 72-hour plan produced — the as-found photos, the listing screenshot, the written order, the dated report, the vendor's replies. The buyer who documented first and negotiated second walks into that process with the file already made. The thresholds and windows above are held in our published recourse dataset; wallet payments funded by card are the unresolved case — ask your issuer rather than assuming.
