How the machine actually works.

The blind box is a beautifully simple machine, and most of the money lost around it is lost by not understanding three of its parts: the series, the secret, and the case. This is the whole mechanism in one page, every number sourced. Last updated 2026-08-30.

The short answer: a series holds six or twelve openly listed regulars plus one hidden secret at long printed odds (commonly reported 1:72 / 1:144; your box's printed rate is authoritative). Odds are per-box averages — twelve boxes at 1:72 give ≈15%, not near-certainty. Sealed-case allocation may differ from loose boxes, and unverifiable “case fresh” or “guaranteed secret” claims are seller claims, not mechanics.

How does a Labubu blind box work?

You buy a sealed box from a themed series without knowing which figure is inside. A series commonly contains six or twelve openly listed regular designs plus one hidden secret that appears at long printed odds — most commonly reported at 1:72 for six-figure series and 1:144 for twelve-figure series. The box tells you the series and the odds; opening it tells you the figure. The randomness is the product.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, the lineup art on the box is the contract: the regulars are knowable, the secret is not pictured in your pull expectations. Second, everything a seller adds on top of the sealed box — “weighed”, “case fresh”, “guaranteed” — is a claim about information the mechanism is designed to hide, which is why such claims deserve the burden of proof.

What do the printed odds actually mean for me?

1:72 means one secret per 72 boxes on average — not that box 72 must contain one, and not that a dozen boxes make it likely. At 1:72, twelve independent boxes give roughly a 15% chance of at least one secret; a 50% chance takes 50 boxes and a 90% chance takes 165. The calculator on the rarity page does this math for any printed rate, and the box's own printed number is always the one to use.

The full reported-rates table with per-row sources — including collab series at 1:120–1:168 and glow variants reported as low as 1:720 — is on the rarity page, and published as a CC-BY dataset machines can read.

Does buying a whole sealed case guarantee the secret?

Not by the printed odds alone, and case allocation claims deserve care. Collector guides report that sealed-case allocation can be structured rather than fully random, but we could not verify a universal rule across series and years — which is exactly why our probability math models loose, independently drawn boxes and says so. If a seller guarantees a secret from a case, the burden of proof is theirs; a guarantee at bargain prices is a counterfeit signal, not generosity.

Is chasing a secret worth it financially?

Do the arithmetic before the feelings: at commonly reported rates, the expected cost of pulling a secret yourself is dozens of boxes' worth of retail — which is why sold-out secrets trade above retail secondhand (DemandSage documents the market level, 2026). Decide what the chase itself is worth to you as entertainment, set the budget on that, and treat any listing that shortcuts the math — cheap guaranteed secrets — as the counterfeit signal it is. We publish odds and checks, not financial advice.

Where the mechanics create traps

  • The gambler's ledger. Per-box odds don't accumulate the way it feels like they should. Twelve boxes at 1:72 is 15%, not “almost there” — and box thirteen is still 1:72.
  • Information claims on a sealed product. Weighing, shaking, “case position” lore: some of it circulates as community craft, none of it is verifiable at purchase. Priced-in certainty from an unverifiable source is exactly what a counterfeit seller sells.
  • Certainty at a discount. The only honest ways to certainty are pulling it yourself (expensive by expectation) or buying it opened at the market's price. Anyone offering certainty below those two prices is answered by the checklist.
